Three Rungs, Three Worked Examples

A sentiment probe, an activation patch, and a counterfactual about one forward pass: three diagnostic questions locate each piece of evidence on Pearl’s causal ladder, show what it supports, and name what it would take to climb a rung.

Do this first: pick a rung below—the three cards are clickable tabs—then run its experiment: the dimmed verdict cards unlock when you do. Each rung is one level of causal evidence: rung 1 observes correlations, rung 2 intervenes (Pearl’s do-operator: externally set a variable), rung 3 asks what would have happened in one observed case. The paper writes the rungs as L1, L2 and L3.

✓ What rung 1 supports

“Sentiment is linearly decodable from layer 12.”

✗ What rung 1 does not support

“The model represents sentiment at layer 12” or “Layer 12 computes sentiment.”

Decodability ≠ encoding; the information may be present but unused by the model’s own computation.

To move this claim up a rung, you need: to set the variable instead of watching it. Write a value into layer 12 and see whether the model’s output changes.

✓ What rung 2 supports

“Intervening on head 9.6 changes IOI behavior under these tested conditions.”

✗ What rung 2 does not support

“Head 9.6 is the IOI mechanism” (sufficiency ≠ necessity; other paths may exist) or “This finding will hold on all IOI-like prompts” (requires transportability testing).

Transportability: whether a causal finding established on one prompt distribution carries over to another.

To move this claim up a rung, you need: for a claim about activations on one prompt, nothing more. With the input given and greedy decoding, the patched rerun of prompt A is the counterfactual for prompt A (under sampling, keep the recorded seed). For a claim about a concept, such as “head 9.6 moves the name,” you need evidence that the head’s activation is that concept: an aligned, identified variable.

Diagnostic question · Rung 3 Is the query anchored to a specific observation?

The Eiffel Tower counterfactual: abduction → action → prediction

Observed run: “The Eiffel Tower is in” → “Paris.” The question is about this run: what would the model have output had a(5) taken the value it takes on “The Colosseum is in”? Pearl’s (2009) recipe answers it in three steps: abduction (infer whatever the observation leaves unknown), action (set a(5)), prediction (rerun with what step (i) found held fixed).

Decoding:

Greedy decoding takes the top token. Sampling adds random noise to the logits and then takes the top token (the Gumbel-max trick, which draws from the softmax); the seed fixes the noise.

Tutorial illustration of the paper’s worked example; the sampling toy uses five made-up logits per run and 2,000 seeded noise draws, computed on load. Not a paper experiment and not a real model.

✓ What rung 3 supports

“For this specific forward pass, had a(5) taken the Colosseum value, the model would have output ‘Rome.’”

✗ What rung 3 does not support

Generalisations to other inputs (requires separate analysis or transportability assumptions) or mechanism-level claims like “layer 5 encodes location.”

A claim about a concept, such as “the model stores the location fact here,” needs a(5) to be that concept, and the rerun cannot verify that: it tells you what this run would have output, not what a(5) stands for.

To move the location claim onto this rung, you need: about a(5), nothing more under greedy decoding, and the recorded seed under sampling for a one-token answer; about the location fact, evidence that a(5) is that concept (an aligned, identified variable), which no rerun on this prompt supplies.

What this shows

Each rung of Pearl’s causal ladder answers a different question, and the hierarchy is cumulative upward: associational evidence does not support interventional claims (Pearl, 2009; Bareinboim et al., 2022). The three scenes re-create the paper’s worked examples. The probe establishes that sentiment is decodable by an external classifier—the model is only observed, so the evidence stays on rung 1 no matter how causal the surrounding language sounds. The patch establishes that head 9.6 causally contributes under the tested conditions—an externally controlled manipulation, hence rung 2, but sufficiency under one intervention is not the mechanism.

The rung-3 stepper shows where the third rung adds something and where it does not. A counterfactual is anchored to one observed forward pass, and Pearl’s abduction–action–prediction recipe answers it. With the input given and greedy decoding, the forward pass leaves nothing unknown, so abduction is empty and the patched rerun is the counterfactual about a(5): for that input, the rung-2 rerun and the rung-3 answer are one computation. Rung 3 differs in two places. Under sampling with the seed lost, the noise must be inferred from the token the run produced, and the answer becomes a distribution that differs from a fresh-seed rerun; a recorded seed makes it one token again. And a claim about a concept, such as “layer 5 encodes location,” needs a(5) to be that concept, which no rerun can verify.

What the paper says

“Decodability ≠ encoding; the information may be present but unused by the model’s own computation.” — Appendix, “Locating an Interpretability Method on the Causal Ladder” (rung 1 example)
“The method establishes that intervening on specific components changes the model’s output (mediation), but storage language implies an internal representation that is the fact—a counterfactual claim about necessity and specificity.” — Appendix, “Calibration Rationales,” Pattern 2: knowledge localisation → storage language

Try this

  1. Take an interpretability claim you know well—“feature X mediates behaviour Y”—and ask the three diagnostic questions in order. Which rung does the evidence occupy, and which rung does the language assert?
  2. In the rung-3 scene, switch decoding to “sampling · seed lost” and run the three steps. Why does keeping only the seeds that produced “Paris” give “Paris” a larger share than the fresh-seed rerun does? What would you have logged to get back to one counterfactual token?
  3. With a partner: one of you defends “head 9.6 is the IOI mechanism,” the other may answer only with the rung-2 verdict cards. Then agree on the experiment that would strengthen the claim (necessity tests, transportability tests, or both).
